# Managing the AI Transition: Overshoot, Non-Replacement, and Adaptive Policy

**Author:** Vijay Suresh  
**Status:** Foundational working paper / preprint  
**Date:** May 2026

[![Zenodo DOI](https://zenodo.org/badge/DOI/10.5281/zenodo.20078381.svg)](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20078381)

## Overview

This paper argues that AI-driven labor disruption should be treated not only as a static substitution or automation-tax problem, but as a **dynamic transition-control problem**.

The central concern is the adjustment path: AI adoption can move faster than workers, firms, demand, governance, and policy institutions can adapt. Even if the long-run equilibrium is productive, the transition can still generate avoidable troughs in labor demand, hiring, entry-level opportunity, firm capacity, and aggregate demand.

The paper develops several reusable concepts:

- **Transition overshoot** — the temporary path can fall below the socially efficient transition path even if the long-run equilibrium is beneficial.
- **Non-replacement mechanism** — effective labor demand can shrink through attrition, delayed backfill, fewer hires, and reduced entry-level intake rather than only through visible layoffs.
- **Capability-deployment gap** — benchmark AI capability is not the same as reliable production substitutability after verification, integration, liability, observability, and error costs.
- **Adaptive dampening** — intervention intensity should respond to transition stress and relax as the system stabilizes, rather than remain static.
- **Capital-market growth-pressure cascade** — AI-linked workforce compression may diffuse through investor expectations, managerial imitation, and efficiency signaling.

## Evidence status

The paper includes a calibrated dynamic simulation and a public-data JOLTS exercise using U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics industry data through March 2026. The public-data evidence is intentionally presented as **suggestive rather than causally decisive**. Broad AI-exposed sectors show lower post-2022 hires/openings ratios, but the paper does not claim that the industry-level difference-in-differences exercise establishes broad AI-caused employment decline.

The contribution is therefore best read as a framework, measurement agenda, and policy architecture disciplined by first-stage public evidence.

## Replication and extensions

The paper lays out a stronger future empirical program using firm-level AI adoption, payroll, job postings, entry-level hiring, separations/backfills, revenue-per-employee, and event-study or hazard-model designs. Replications, critiques, extensions, and improved datasets are welcome.
